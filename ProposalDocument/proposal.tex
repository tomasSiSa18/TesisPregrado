\subsection{Differential Privacy}

Differential privacy describes a promise that is made by a data holder or curator to a data subject: "You will not be affected, adversely or otherwise, by allowing your data to be used in any study or analysis, no matter what other studies, data sets, or information sources, are available".[1] In other words differential privacy is the process that either a data curator or a data holder must do in order to assure that a single individual's data can be extracted from the study as a whole.

\begin{definition}
    ($\epsilon$, $\delta$)-Differential privacy [1]. A randomized algorithm $\mathcal{M}$ will provide ($\epsilon$, $\delta$)-Differential privacy (henceforth ($\epsilon$, $\delta$)-DP) if for any two outputs on two different databases D and D' differing by at most one record, and for all outputs $A \subseteq Range(O)$:

    $$Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) \in O] \leq e^{\epsilon}Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') \in O]$$
\end{definition}

In this study we are going to mostly focus around the concept of \textbf{Central differential privacy} which states that it is the responsibility of the curator (i.e a central party that recieves all the raw data from the participants and adds privacy to them) to assure that the data is going to be differentially private.


\subsection{Quantum Information and Computation}

Quantum computing significantly advances computational capabilities, performing exponentially faster computation than clasical computers.[2] In order to understand some concepts of Quantum differential privacy it is important to define the following notions:

\begin{definition}
    Quantum states: A quantum state of the system X is described by a density matrix $\rho$ whose entries are complex numbers and whose indices (for both its rows and columns) have been place in correspondence with the classical state set $\Sigma$
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    Mixed state: A mixed state of a quantum system is represented by and ensemble

    $$\mathbb{E} = {(p_1,\ket{\psi_1}),...,(p_k,\ket{\psi_k})}$$

    which in turn can be represented by the density operator

    $$\rho_{\mathbb{E}} = \Sigma_{i}p_i\ket{\psi_i}\bra{\psi_i}$$

    thus the resulting $\rho_{\mathbb{E}}$ will be the density matrix representation of the ensemble.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    Quantum Channel: A quantum channel describes a linear mapping over density matrices. 
\end{definition}

Quantum channels are important as they are the generalization of an operation over a quantum state. This generalization is necessary as in quantum differential privacy the noise introduced into the system will be destructive over the quantum state and thus not representative of a more specific definition of operation

\begin{definition}
    Trace distance: It is the distance between two density operators and it is defined by:

    $$\tau(\rho, \sigma) = \frac{1}{2}Tr\abs{\rho - \sigma}$$

    where $Tr(x)$ is the trace of the density matrix $x$
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    Quantum measurement: It is the way to extract information from a quantum system. Matemathically, a measurement is modelled as a set of matrices $M = {K_m}$ with the condition of 

    $$\Sigma_m K^{\dagger}_{m}K_{m} = I$$

    with $I$ being the identity of the density operator (i.e for a qbit $I = 2\times2$ identity matrix)

\end{definition}

when talking about quantum differential privacy below it won't really matter what the quantum state colapses into thus we can use the notion of a POVM (Positive Operator-Valued Measure) which is a simplification over the measurement where we get that $M = {M_m}$ with $M_m = K^{\dagger}_{m}K_{m}$ $\forall m$ since $p_m = Tr(K_{m} \rho K^{\dagger}_{m}) = Tr(M_m\rho)$

\subsection{Quantum Differential Privacy}

When understanding classical differential privacy one might realize the possibility for it to be applied as well to quantum information. The promise that classical DP curator makes to the data holder does not change when in the quantum setting however the means to reach that promise do

\begin{definition}
    (Quantum differential privacy []). Let $d \in (0,1]$ and $\epsilon,\delta > 0$ be three constants. A quantum operation $\mathcal{E}$ is $(\epsilon, \delta)$-differentially private if for every POVM $M = {M_m}$, for all outputs $S \subseteq Out(M)$ and for all inputs $\rho, \sigma$ such that $\tau(\rho, \sigma)\leq d$, it holds that:

    $$Pr[\mathcal{E}(\rho)\in_{M}S] \leq e^{\epsilon} Pr[\mathcal{E}(\sigma)\in_{M}S]$$
\end{definition}
